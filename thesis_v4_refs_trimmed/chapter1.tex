\section{Why procurement disputes are worth modelling}
\label{sec:context}

UK public bodies spend around \pounds300 billion a year on goods, works and
services \cite{cabinetoffice2020}, and in 2023 the rulebook changed. The
Procurement Act 2023 \cite{pa2023} replaced a patchwork of EU-derived
regulations with a single regime built around four statutory objectives in
section 12: value for money, maximising public benefit, transparency, and
integrity. It also tightened what happens to losing bidders: section 50 requires an
assessment summary explaining how a tender was scored, and section 51
opens an eight-working-day standstill from the award notice's publication,
during which the contract cannot be signed. A supplier who thinks the
evaluation went wrong has that window to raise it, and thirty days from
knowledge of the breach to start proceedings in the Technology and
Construction Court.

Those deadlines make this problem interesting rather than merely legal: the
risk screen (\S\ref{sec:risk-screen}) acts before a dispute exists, the
negotiation simulator (\S\ref{sec:orchestration}) on one already raised.
Nearly all the leverage and information asymmetry sits in the first
fortnight, before anyone has instructed a solicitor: the authority knows
exactly what its evaluation panel did, the losing bidder only what the
assessment summary tells it. Once the automatic suspension kicks in and
the case reaches the TCC, both sides are locked into a process taking four
months to two years across this corpus.

This project was carried out with Fusion21, a social enterprise running
procurement frameworks for housing providers, local authorities and NHS
bodies. Fusion21 supplied four internal documents, Docs 1--4: each party's
interests and drivers, the escalation ladder to litigation, the
negotiation theory applied, and three worked examples. Doc 4 makes a point
shaping the whole project: litigated cases are not typical, since most
disputes settle before proceedings begin, so every judgment here is a
survivor of that filtering, and any claim on it inherits that bias.

\section{Subject area and related work}
\label{sec:related}

The technical territory is multi-agent LLM simulation: several instances of
a language model, each assigned a role, interests and a private fallback
position, acting out a structured multi-party interaction.

\subsection*{Multi-agent LLM frameworks}

Once instruction-following models could follow few-shot task specifications
\cite{brown2020language} and expose intermediate reasoning \cite{wei2022chain},
the prompt became a programming surface for role-differentiated multi-agent
interaction; MetaGPT \cite{hong2024metagpt} argues what stops such conversation
collapsing into noise is encoding standard operating procedures into the
prompts, exactly what the Court agent's verification protocol
(\S\ref{sec:court-design}) does, closer to ReAct's interleaving of reasoning
with a defined action space \cite{yao2023react} than open-ended chat.
Orchestration runs on LangGraph \cite{langgraph}, an explicit state graph,
defended in \S\ref{sec:orchestration}.

\subsection*{LLMs as negotiators}

Negotiation is a good testbed because it comes with structure and an outcome:
LLMs have bargained over a toy resource \cite{fu2023negotiation}, been
benchmarked across resource-allocation and ultimatum games
\cite{bianchi2024negotiation}, and negotiated multi-party scenarios scored
against private constraints \cite{abdelnabi2024negotiation}. All three define
payoff within the game itself, so correctness is decidable; a procurement
dispute's governing framework sits outside the simulation, in statute and
case law, so the ``correct'' outcome is whatever a court would have held,
usually unknown.

The agents reason with Fisher and Ury's \emph{Getting to Yes}
\cite{fisher2011getting} (interests over positions, know your BATNA) and
Raiffa on asymmetric negotiation \cite{raiffa1982art}. Doc 3 adds a
constraint shaping the system's whole outcome vocabulary: in award-stage
disputes you cannot split the difference, since remedies are fixed by
statute --- set-aside, a declaration of ineffectiveness, and damages ---
descending from the EU Remedies Directive as amended in 2007
\cite{remediesdirective} and carried into the Procurement Act 2023, the
fixed menu Arrowsmith \cite{arrowsmith2014law} treats a challenge as argued
into. A negotiation simulator here cannot invent a settlement shape, a
stronger constraint than most negotiation benchmarks impose. Genn
\cite{genn2009judging} argues civil disputes overwhelmingly settle rather
than being adjudicated, and an early, cheap signal informs settlement
rather than predicting a judgment nobody will seek --- Fusion21's Doc 4
makes the same observation about procurement specifically.

\subsection*{Legal reasoning and the fabrication problem}

On legal benchmarks, language models do reasonably well: GPT-4 passed the
Uniform Bar Examination \cite{katz2024gpt4}, and LegalBench
\cite{guha2023legalbench} showed real, if uneven, legal reasoning across 162
tasks. The evidence that matters more cuts the other way: Dahl et al.\
\cite{dahl2024legal} found legal hallucination rates of 58--82\% on
verifiable questions about federal cases, and Magesh et al.\
\cite{magesh2024hallucination} measured hallucination above 17\% even in
commercial retrieval-augmented legal tools built specifically to avoid it
\cite{ji2023survey} --- fluent, confident, wrong output is the default
failure mode here, not a rare edge case.

A plausible-looking compliance finding on a procurement dispute is
actively harmful precisely because it is persuasive, unless something
separately guarantees the numbers and provisions it cites came from the
case at hand. Retrieval \cite{lewis2020rag} was the obvious candidate;
Section~\ref{sec:alternatives} explains why it was scoped out and what was
built instead.

\section{The gap this project addresses}
\label{sec:gap}

Multi-agent negotiation research is typically scored against a payoff
function the environment defines; legal-LLM research against benchmark
answers a human has already labelled. A simulated procurement dispute has
neither luxury: the artefact hands down a legal judgment on facts a real
court either decided differently, decided on a different question, or
never decided. So the question this project organised itself around was
not \emph{can agents negotiate a procurement dispute}, the easy part, but
how you evaluate what they produce and stop them inventing the evidence
they reason from.

\section{Aim, research questions and objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

\textbf{Aim.} To build and evaluate a multi-agent LLM system simulating
procurement dispute resolution under the Procurement Act 2023, modelling
each party's interests, constraints and fallback position, and to
establish an evaluation methodology suited to a domain where ground truth
is scarce and fabrication is the main risk.

The research questions, carried over from the project plan and sharpened
by the work, are:

\begin{description}
 \item[RQ1] Without fine-tuning, can prompt engineering and structured context
 injection alone produce agents that reliably represent each party's distinct
 interests, legal constraints and BATNA?
 \item[RQ2] Which negotiation protocol lets autonomous agents reach legally
 grounded resolutions, and which single component is doing the work?
 \item[RQ3] Can an LLM court agent verify arithmetic when a case supplies one,
 refuse to invent one when it doesn't, and does that constraint survive
 dispute shapes it was never designed against?
 \item[RQ4] Given that most real disputes settle confidentially with no
 ground-truth label, how should outcomes be evaluated for reliability,
 fabrication and alignment with what happened?
\end{description}

These six objectives give Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} its structure and
are assessed against the evidence in Table~\ref{tab:objectives}.

\begin{description}
  \item[O1] Specify three negotiating agents plus a non-negotiating summariser
    against Fusion21's own interest taxonomies.
  \item[O2] Build a deterministic, inspectable orchestration with
    schema-validated output.
  \item[O3] Design a court agent that verifies conditionally, not by
    default.
  \item[O4] Build an ingestion path from real documents into structured
    scenarios.
  \item[O5] Assemble a verified corpus of real UK judgments, evaluated
    against explicit baselines and ablations.
  \item[O6] Deliver something Fusion21 can use, which became a pre-award
    risk screen rather than the simulator.
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{A conditional verification protocol for an LLM adjudicator}
 (\S\ref{sec:court-design}): exact arithmetic only when a formula and every
 input it needs are present; invented values refused even hedged as
 illustrative. Four prompt revisions, each answering a measured failure.
 \item \textbf{An evaluation methodology for a domain with almost no ground
 truth} (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}): agreement with real dispositions, four
 fabrication screens, structural-compliance instrumentation, a controlled
 prompt ablation, and three baselines.
 \item \textbf{An outcome-leakage audit and fix} (\S\ref{sec:agreement}): six
 of fourteen merits scenarios were found stating the real disposition in the
 text agents reason over, rewritten to remove it, and every accuracy figure
 re-run against the corrected, leak-free set.
 \item \textbf{A negative result bounding the architecture claim}
 (\S\ref{sec:baselines}): on leak-free data, a majority-class heuristic, a
 naive zero-shot prompt and the full pipeline all converge on 5/6 directional
 accuracy, while removing the court agent deadlocks every run --- the
 architecture earns its keep on resolution mechanics, not accuracy.
 \item \textbf{Four distinct failure modes}, on named cases
 (\S\ref{sec:fabrication}): numeric fabrication (13 confirmed instances, all
 invented inputs with correct arithmetic), narrative-premise fabrication,
 scenario-attribution fabrication, and legal-judgment divergence; hardening
 the adjudicator against the first does nothing about the rest.
 \item \textbf{A verified corpus of 23 real UK procurement judgments}, rendered as
 structured scenarios, each disposition checked individually.
 \item \textbf{A pre-award challenge risk screen} (\S\ref{sec:risk-screen}), traced
 rule by rule to Fusion21's own practical-considerations tables, the only
 part of this system with an observable ground truth.
\end{enumerate}
