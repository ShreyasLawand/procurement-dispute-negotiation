\section{What the research questions turned out to have as answers}

\textbf{RQ1, on prompt engineering without fine-tuning.} Yes, with a caveat
that surfaced only under testing. Agents specified through role prompts and
injected context sustain distinct, role-appropriate positions: the
authority concedes rarely on opening and about half the time thereafter,
the bidder far less, and each reports the opposite kind of outcome against
its own declared fallback. But a prompt developed against one dispute shape
does not generalise to another --- confronted with a case where nobody was
ever scored, both agents invented a scoring dispute, the only script they
had.

\textbf{RQ2, on which part of the protocol is load-bearing.} The
adjudicator, and essentially nothing else: removing it deadlocks every run.
Close to trivial in hindsight, since nothing else in the graph can set the
resolution flag, but it remains the one unambiguous architectural finding,
worth more than the accuracy figures, which do not distinguish this system
from a single prompt.

\textbf{RQ3, on constraining an LLM adjudicator's arithmetic.} Partially:
the boundary is now mapped as a real, quantified failure rate, not assumed
clean. Thirteen confirmed Court-originated fabrications across four cases
(Bromcom, Parkingeye, Alstom, Woods) share the same shape --- invented input
numbers with correct arithmetic on them, which is harder to catch than
invented arithmetic itself --- and a fourth, rarer mode surfaced only on
close reading: the Court fabricating what its own scenario said, confirmed
in 4 of 23 systematically-checked instances. The discipline constrains what
the adjudicator computes, not what premise it computes on or claims to have
been given, and the client's own scoring-table arithmetic defect resisted
three prompt revisions regardless.

\textbf{RQ4, on evaluating in the absence of ground truth.} A stack of
partial checks with explicit sample sizes, and a refusal to collapse them
into one score: an architecture check, because a system can agree with
reality for reasons unrelated to its design; fabrication screens, because a
correct conclusion from invented evidence looks identical from outside to
an honest one; and a leakage audit, because a scenario can hand over the
answer. It also needs to report negative results, since the two most
informative findings, the three-way baseline tie and the readability
shortfall, both count against the artefact.

\section{Reflection on the approach}

The complexity that mattered was not the system's scope --- five agent
types, an orchestrator, an ingestion path, a risk screen, a verified
corpus, ten analysis scripts --- but that the domain punishes what
generative models are best at: a model asked about a procurement dispute
produces something fluent, structured and legally shaped, and the harder
the case, the more confident it sounds. Almost every methodological
decision in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} traces back to that:
schema-validated output, compliance counters, a source-length guard, and a
verification protocol whose purpose is getting the adjudicator to say ``I
cannot compute this'' when it cannot.

The most useful bugs came from documents I had not chosen. A board
demonstration produced an award letter and scoring table exposing a
genuine arithmetic defect --- the Court re-applying an already-applied
percentage weighting --- which three prompt revisions failed to fix and
which stays open. Choosing my own test inputs had selected for failures
already known; testing on unchosen documents sooner would have surfaced
this.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

Three directions follow from findings reported above rather than from
speculation.

\textbf{Verify AbbVie at the scale that resolved Alstom.} The Alstom
ablation moved from suggestive ($n=8$, $p=0.077$) to conclusive ($n=30$,
$p=3.96\times10^{-5}$) simply by adding runs. AbbVie's flip from correct to
wrong once its leak was removed rests on a single $n=8$ batch, and deserves
the same scale-up before its miss is treated as settled rather than
provisional.

\textbf{Close the smaller gaps documented above:} grounding citations
against the legislation, checking negotiating-agent premises the way
arithmetic is checked, and extending the remedy vocabulary beyond scoring
challenges, the natural next steps for the citation, premise-fabrication
and vocabulary gaps this report already quantifies (\S\ref{sec:citation},
\S\ref{sec:fabrication}, \S\ref{sec:agreement}).

\textbf{Calibrate the risk screen against real outcomes.} This is the only
thing this project asks of Fusion21, and the highest-value item on the
list. Anonymised pre-action data from their member base (the profile
fields the screen requests, plus whether a challenge followed) would let
the severity-weighted heuristic be replaced or validated by a fitted
model, and the risk score reported as a calibrated probability rather than
an ordering --- the only component whose central claim is falsifiable,
which is why it deserves the data most.

The broader conclusion is narrower than the one I expected to write: a
multi-agent LLM system can conduct a procedurally coherent procurement
dispute negotiation, and can be constrained not to invent the arithmetic it
reasons from, but cannot be shown to reason better than a single
well-posed prompt, and can still be led into confident nonsense by a
premise it was handed rather than one it worked out. The value here sits
less in the simulator than in the evidence of what such a system does when
nobody is checking, and in the demonstration that finding that out
requires screens built to catch a system wrong in a fluent, plausible way.
