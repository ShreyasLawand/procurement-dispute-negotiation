\chapter{Evaluation and Reflection}
\label{ch:evaluation}

\section{Designing an evaluation when there is no ground truth}
\label{sec:eval-design}

Accuracy against real outcomes is the obvious evaluation, and mostly
unavailable: Fusion21's Doc 4 states most procurement disputes settle before
proceedings, so a published judgment is already a biased minority, and of
the 23 here, nine were decided on an interim application where the merits
were never reached. Any single accuracy number over that set would be small
and misleading.

The evaluation is instead a stack of partial checks, each answering a
question the others can't, reported with the sample size that produced it,
following the reporting discipline Dodge et al.\
\cite{dodge2019showyourwork} argue for:

\begin{description}
 \item[Structural] well-formed output? Compliance over 2{,}865 responses
 (\S\ref{sec:structural}).
 \item[Agreement] does it agree with reality where knowable? 14 merits
 dispositions (\S\ref{sec:agreement}).
 \item[Architecture] does it earn its complexity? Three baselines, one
 ablation, $n=6$ and $n=18$ (\S\ref{sec:baselines}).
 \item[Prompt] does a change alter judgement or only confidence? Controlled
 V3/V4 ablation, $n=8$--30 per cell, Fisher's exact (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).
 \item[Grounding] is the reasoning grounded in what the model was given?
 Four independent fabrication screens (\S\ref{sec:fabrication}).
 \item[Usability] is the output usable by whoever acts on it? Concession
 dynamics, BATNA self-report, readability (\S\ref{sec:dynamics}).
\end{description}

No single row is sufficient; the fabrication screens exist because a system
can score well on every row above while inventing the evidence it reasons
from. Every figure below comes from an analysis script run over committed
logs, re-run before submission, against a corpus of over 400 completed
negotiations.

\section{The corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}

Twenty-three real UK procurement judgments were researched, individually
verified against BAILII or the National Archives, and written into the
project as dense source texts, then run through the same extraction path a
user upload takes: 14 with a genuine merits disposition, where a court
ruled on the substantive question this system assesses (6 authority wins to
8 losses), and 9 interim-only rulings where the merits were never decided,
so comparing a merits prediction against them is not a check. Only the
merits group supports an agreement claim; the interim group is reported for
completeness and excluded from every accuracy figure here. The full list,
with citations and the further candidates rejected, is in
Appendix~\ref{app:corpus}.

The corpus grew in three stages as the bottleneck shifted from compute ---
local CPU inference took roughly seventeen minutes per negotiation --- to
research and verification, once a tunnelled A10G GPU cut that latency
thirty-fold; the largest expansion added fifteen cases in one session.

\section{Structural reliability}
\label{sec:structural}

Across the 50 batches carrying the instrumentation described in
\S\ref{sec:compliance-method}, 2{,}861 of 2{,}865 structured responses
required no repair: a corpus-wide structural compliance rate of
\textbf{0.9986}. Forty-eight of the 50 batches were clean at 1.00; the worst
scored 0.906 and predates the repair-retry pattern below.

That pattern is itself a finding: adding anti-fabrication and
governing-legislation instructions to the pre-negotiation prompts raised
the rate at which the model dropped a required JSON field, first showing
as three failures in an eight-run batch where the prior corpus had none.
The cause was the instruction's length, not content: a longer, denser
prompt degraded adherence to the output-format instruction at its end, the
positional sensitivity Lu et al.\ \cite{lu2022fantastically} document for
few-shot prompts, here in a format instruction rather than example
ordering. The fix was a single repair retry re-sending the message with
the validation error appended --- worth naming for anyone hardening
prompts against a small model: \emph{adding an instruction has a cost paid
elsewhere in the same prompt}, invisible unless something counts it.

\section{Agreement with real dispositions}
\label{sec:agreement}

On the 14 cases with a genuine merits disposition, the simulated outcome
direction matches the real one in \textbf{12 of 14}, and this figure is
leak-free in the strongest sense: not merely measured and caveated, but
found and fixed. Auditing all 23 cached scenarios
(\texttt{analyze\_outcome\_leakage.py}) found six of the fourteen merits
scenarios stating the real court's disposition inside the text the agents
reason over --- Bromcom's description, for instance, contained ``the court
found in favour of Bromcom on all three grounds'', not procedural context
but the answer, and the Court prompt separately instructs the agent not to
contradict a stated disposition.\label{sec:leakage} Those six were
rewritten to state only the facts of the dispute, and every figure in this
chapter was re-run against the corrected, leak-free 14-case set.

Two misses remain, for two different reasons, and neither is a bug. On
Turning Point v Norfolk County Council, the simulated Court's reasoning is
almost verbatim the real claimant's losing argument, that the authority
should have sought clarification of a caveated tender before rejecting it,
and the real judge rejected exactly that: nothing was invented, and the
reasoning engages the real facts throughout, but the model reached a
different legal conclusion on a genuinely arguable point, a category of
error \S\ref{sec:fabrication} separates from fabrication. On AbbVie v NHS
England, once its leaked disposition was removed, the Court engaged the
real mechanics of the Dummy Price Mechanism genuinely and concluded it was
structurally discriminatory, where the real court held that AbbVie's own
pricing choices, not the mechanism, explained its loss --- a real
prediction miss, not a fabrication, and the clearest evidence that the
leak-free figure is measuring something harder than compliance.

Two further caveats attach to otherwise-correct directions. On Faraday the
direction is correct but the reasoning is not trustworthy, for the
premise-fabrication reasons \S\ref{sec:fabrication} gives. On Consultant
Connect the direction is correct but the remedy shape is wrong, since the
real court imposed financial penalties on three NHS bodies that this
system's vocabulary cannot express, so ``re-evaluation'' stands in for ``the
authority was at fault''. The same gap shows on Faraday, whose real remedy
was a declaration of ineffectiveness, and on Woods, where the court itself
determined the corrected winner rather than merely ordering the scoring
redone --- the vocabulary was built for scoring challenges, and it shows.

Across all 299 runs the outcome distribution is heavily skewed: 241
re-evaluation (80.6\%), 31 no remedy (10.4\%), 17 deadlock (5.7\%), 9
disclosure ordered (3.0\%), and one run outside the closed vocabulary
(``request for additional information'', from an early four-run batch) ---
the kind of drift the downstream module is designed to raise on rather than
average away. Median resolution takes one round; the maximum is five. The
skew toward re-evaluation is what makes the majority-class baseline in
\S\ref{sec:baselines} a demanding floor, not a straw man.

\section{Baselines and ablations: does the architecture earn itself?}
\label{sec:baselines}

This is the question an examiner asks first, tested head-on across the six
cases where a merits disposition then existed. Reporting a
score with no comparable floor is the failure Dodge et al.\
\cite{dodge2019showyourwork} single out, so three baselines were built: a
majority-class heuristic with no model call, a zero-shot prompt deliberately
\emph{not} hardened the way the Court prompt is, and an ablation replacing
the Court node with one that cannot resolve.

The headline result is now flat, not just negative. \textbf{On leak-free
data, the majority-class heuristic, the naive zero-shot prompt, and the full
pipeline all score 5 of 6 and converge on the identical miss, AbbVie} ---
where the Court engages the Dummy Price Mechanism's real structure and
still reaches the wrong conclusion. No claim about the architecture's value
can rest on accuracy: three methods spanning zero, one, and many rounds of
reasoning are indistinguishable on this measure. A second check applied the
Court agent's own grounding screen to all 30 zero-shot reasoning texts to
see whether the unhardened prompt fabricated more: zero ungrounded numbers,
a genuine null result. The architecture's defence rests entirely on the
ablation that follows.

Removing the Court agent produces deadlock in \textbf{all 18 runs, uniformly
on every case} --- not sampling luck but an architectural necessity, since
nothing else in the graph can decide the parties have converged. An
ablation that inferred resolution from conciliatory dialogue would have
defeated the point, which is that nothing here resolves without
adjudication. The defensible claim is about mechanism, not accuracy: the
pipeline produces a modelled negotiation with declared interests, BATNAs,
rounds of concession and a court reviewing the actual exchange, none of
which exists in a single call and all of which the downstream components
depend on.

\section{The V3/V4 prompt ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

Replacing the Court agent's three-line guiding-principles block with Doc 1's
six judiciary interest categories is a single-variable change, and its
effect is consistent. Every cell is eight repeated runs of one fixed
scenario, so the spread within a cell sizes the sampling noise rather than
case variation. Across every case where V3 deadlocks, V4 reaches zero
deadlock and resolves in roughly half the rounds (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
Reaching 1.00 on every case is not automatically the better result, as the
rest of this section shows.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\footnotesize
\caption[Ablation significance]{V3/V4 resolution rates, Fisher's exact
tests \cite{fisher1922interpretation}. Four cases at $n=8$ reach no
significance, consistent with ``not distinguishable from noise at this
sample size'' \cite{dror2018hitchhiker}; Alstom* was bumped to
$n{\approx}30$ after a power check and is the exception.}
\label{tab:ablation}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Case} & \textbf{V3} & \textbf{V4} & \textbf{V3 avg.\ rounds} & \textbf{Fisher $p$} \\
\midrule
Lancashire Care & 0.875 & 1.000 & 2.75 & 1.000 \\
Faraday & 0.750 & 1.000 & 3.00 & 0.467 \\
Parkingeye & 0.875 & 1.000 & 1.50 & 1.000 \\
Alstom* ($n{\approx}30$) & 0.310 & 0.857 & 4.52 & $3.96\times10^{-5}$ \\
Woods & 1.000 & 1.000 & 1.00 & 1.000 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Only Alstom pulls the suggested direction all the way to significance. The
pattern is real and holds consistently across five cases, matching the
mechanism predicted from the change: Doc 1's fifth judicial category is
efficient use of judicial resources, favouring settlement and narrowing of
issues. On four cases nothing reaches significance at $n=8$ per arm; a
power check showed Alstom's gap was large enough to be worth the extra GPU
time, and at $n{\approx}30$ it is decisive ($p=3.96\times10^{-5}$),
surviving every sensitivity check tried, including excluding or recoding
the Alstom runs independently confirmed as fabricated (worst case
$p=0.0014$, still 37 times below the threshold). On Alstom, V4's
manifest-error detection rate (0.39) is not reliably different from V3's
(0.31) even at this scale ($p=0.585$), so the resolution-rate gap lies
substantially in what happens \emph{after} a finding, not only in whether
one gets made. The honest reading is that V4 changes remedy efficiency, and
on the one case tested at adequate scale, does so in a way that is not
noise --- but the other four remain genuinely unresolved at $n=8$, and ``V4
improves resolution rate'' should not travel as a claim about them without
that qualification.

\section{Fabrication: four distinct failure modes}
\label{sec:fabrication}

This is the project's central concern, and the screens are correspondingly
paranoid: four independent checks run over the corpus
(Table~\ref{tab:fabrication}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\footnotesize
\caption[Fabrication screens]{The four grounding screens, over 299-406 logs
as the corpus grew during this work.}
\label{tab:fabrication}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.6cm}cX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Screen} & \textbf{Result} & \textbf{Reading} \\
\midrule
Numeric grounding (Step 2A/2B) & \textbf{13 confirmed} of 784 screened &
 All Court-originated, all the same shape: invented input numbers, correct
 arithmetic on them. Bromcom, Parkingeye, Alstom (5), Woods (6) \\
Scenario-attribution fabrication & \textbf{4 confirmed} of 23 candidates
 (17.4\%) & The Court asserting its own scenario said something it did not;
 one instance factually backwards from the real disposition \\
Legal citation, regime & 162 of 702 (23.1\%) wrong regime &
 PCR 2015 or the EU Directive cited in a Procurement Act 2023 world \\
Legal citation, s.12 content & 7 of 21 (33.3\%) misattributed &
 Subsection contents swapped, e.g.\ transparency assigned to s.12(1)(a) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The numeric screen extracts every score-shaped number from Court reasoning
and checks it against the scenario description, widened to also catch bare
declarations (``A=80'') and separate a correctly-derived arithmetic result
from its inputs. Screening 784
qualitative-and-numeric assessments together finds \textbf{13 confirmed
Court-originated fabrications} across four cases, and every one shares the
same shape: invented input numbers, with subsequently correct arithmetic
performed on them. On Bromcom the Court opened ``this scenario is
numeric'', invented a complete per-bidder dataset (``let's say the scores
are as follows: Bidder 1: A=80, B=70, C=90\ldots''), and computed correctly
on twelve values that appear nowhere in the case; Woods shows the identical
pattern six times, most explicitly as ``let's assume the original scores
were not provided\ldots a hypothetical score of 60 for EAS and 40 for
Woods''. A reader checking only the arithmetic would find nothing wrong in
any of the 13: the fabrication is entirely upstream of the calculation, in
the inputs the Court invents for itself. Most rounds instead show
disciplined behaviour, worth quoting since the detector fired as designed:

\begin{quote}\itshape\small
``I do not have enough information to perform a calculation myself, as no specific
numbers or formula are provided in the scenario.''
\hfill --- Court agent, Alstom, round 2
\end{quote}

A fourth, rarer mode surfaced only on close reading, not any automated
screen: the Court asserting its own scenario stated something it did not. A
systematic search across all 406 logs for this self-referential pattern
found 23 candidate claims, of which \textbf{4 (17.4\%) are confirmed
false}. Only one is factually backwards from the real disposition --- on
Woods, a round claims the scenario states ``the court found no error and
dismissed the challenge'', appearing nowhere in the text and the opposite
of the real outcome --- the other three fabricate subsidiary details (a
notice claim, a score value, a sub-score dataset) rather than invert the
finding itself. Four instances is too few to establish a cause, but the pattern spans old
and new scenarios and both prompt versions, reading as real and
low-frequency rather than a single anomaly.

The screen's real signal sits further upstream: the negotiating agents
fabricate factual premises, and the Court adopts them. The clearest
instance is Faraday, not a scoring dispute at all: the authority structured
a \pounds125\,m regeneration deal to avoid running a competition, and
Faraday never submitted a bid to be scored. Extraction captured this
correctly, labelling the dispute \texttt{process\_avoidance}; the
negotiating agents did not preserve it. In 3 of 8 V3 runs and 6 of 8 V4
runs the bidder asserts a ``scoring discrepancy between our bid and that of
St Modwen Developments Ltd'', impossible since neither party was scored.
The cause is traceable: every CA and bidder prompt was developed against
scored-bid disputes, with no branch for an unscored case, so confronted
with an unfamiliar type, both fall back on the only script they have.

Four failure modes, then, that a single ``hallucination'' label would
conflate: numeric fabrication (13 confirmed, addressed by the widened
screen); scenario-attribution fabrication (4 confirmed, found only by
systematic search, open); premise fabrication (open, 6 of 8 Faraday runs,
entering before the adjudicator sees it); and judgment divergence (Turning
Point, not a defect). Hardening the adjudicator against the first does
nothing about the second or third, since both enter before its own check
runs.

\subsection{Legal citation}
\label{sec:citation}

Citation is the same discipline in a different field, and holds up far
less well (Table~\ref{tab:fabrication}); both checks were verified against
legislation.gov.uk, and the 519 citations too general to check are
bucketed as unknown rather than passed as correct.

This is the clearest limitation of prompt-only grounding: the
citation-discipline instruction cut fabricated pinpoint citations, but
cannot give the model knowledge of which regime governs a 2018 procurement,
since an 8B model's priors are saturated with pre-2023 material regardless
of the prompt. Retrieval \cite{lewis2020rag} over the legislation is the
right fix, and the strongest argument here for building the layer scoped
out.

\section{Negotiation dynamics and BATNA}
\label{sec:dynamics}

The Contracting Authority's concession rate rises from 0.013 in round one
to 0.470 later, against 0.000 to 0.075 for the Aggrieved Bidder --- an
asymmetry reflecting what a concession means on each side, since a
bidder's only real concession is dropping its challenge. Zero explicit
retractions were found, using a check narrowed after an earlier version
misread ``willing to withdraw our claim'', a settlement offer, as a
retraction.

Self-reported outcome against declared BATNA shows the same asymmetry: the
Contracting Authority beats its BATNA 71\% against 24\% for the Aggrieved
Bidder, matching how the law structures risk on each side --- an
authority that successfully defends its award loses nothing, while a
bidder who wins a re-evaluation has already spent the bid cost and may lose
again. Despite the Summary agent's schema promising output ``a non-lawyer
could understand'', mean Flesch Reading Ease \cite{flesch1948readability}
across all 299 summaries is \textbf{24.7} (``very difficult, graduate
level''), with a mean Flesch--Kincaid grade \cite{kincaid1975derivation} of
\textbf{14.7}.

\section{The risk screen against real cases}
\label{sec:risk-eval}

The screen was run against eight real cases, filling a profile field only
where the judgment states or entails it. Six produced any populated field;
Faraday and Alstom produced none, the finding this section turns on. Two of
the six reach ``medium'' from bidder-side facts alone, Braceurself among
them with a stated 80.25\% against 82.5\% margin --- one of the clearest
cases where a court found the process defective. That co-occurrence is
worth naming, but not a validated correlation given only six cases.

The remaining four land ``low'' despite being real litigated challenges, a
finding about the data, not a failure of the screen: judgments record
legal reasoning, not whether the evaluation panel was procurement-trained
or the audit trail complete, the conditions the rules key on. The module
states an unpopulated profile always scores low, a gap in the input, not a
finding.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Sample size.} Every rate here is a proportion over a modest $N$.
Four of five ablation cells are $n=8$ with no significant result; the
baseline comparison is $n=6$. Alstom, bumped to $n{\approx}30$, is the
exception, its own n=8-vs-n=30 rates moving substantially itself evidence
the smaller cells are underpowered rather than settled. The deadlock
finding is the other exception, holding at 18 of 18 by architectural
necessity, not sampling luck.

\textbf{Structural mismatch.} Nine of 23 cases were decided at an interim
stage this system cannot model, excluded from every accuracy figure here.
Outcome leakage was found and fixed for this corpus (\S\ref{sec:agreement}),
but stripping a disposition at ingestion needs to run on any future
upload, not just once.

\textbf{Single model and corpus bias.} Everything runs on Llama 3.1 8B at
fixed per-role temperatures, so nothing here separates this architecture
from this model under these prompts. The corpus is doubly filtered ---
litigated cases are already a minority of disputes, and verification
narrowed it further --- and the merits group's 6-to-8 win/loss balance came
from deliberately seeking authority-win cases, itself a selection choice.

\textbf{Detector limits.} The numeric screen is string-presence matching
against the scenario description, not a fact-checker; the Faraday
premise-fabrication count comes from a phrase search and is a lower bound;
the BATNA classifier is a keyword heuristic. Each limit is stated plainly in
its own module.
